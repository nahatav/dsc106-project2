Is the gap in child mortality between countries closing? It depends how you measure it

[Member 1], [Member 2], [Member 3] and [Member 4] [email1]@ucsd.edu, [email2]@ucsd.edu, [email3]@ucsd.edu, [email4]@ucsd.edu

Between 1960 and 2019, every one of the 112 countries with World Bank under-five mortality records at both ends cut its rate, and the median country went from 149 to 15 deaths per 1,000 live births. Whether that progress narrowed the gap between countries depends on how the gap is measured. We built one visualization arguing that it did and one arguing that it did not. Both start from the same 112 countries and the same 10th- and 90th-percentile rates, and neither contains a false number. Measured as a difference, the gap fell 75 percent. Measured as a ratio, it doubled.

Proposition

The gap in under-five mortality rates between countries is narrowing.

1The case for a narrowing gap

Measure the gap as a difference, and it collapses. In 1960 the country at the 90th percentile lost 285 of every 1,000 children before age 5, and the country at the 10th percentile lost 30, a gap of 255. By 2019 those rates were 66.9 and 3.5, a gap of 63. Every country in the group improved along the way.

1.1How we made the FOR visualization

Each decision is scored from −2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest).

  1. Compare the same 112 countries in every year

    We kept only the 112 countries with a rate in both 1960 and 2019, so the trend cannot come from countries entering the data. That is the textbook choice, but it is not neutral. The 81 countries left out include Nigeria, Niger, Mali, Chad, Somalia and South Sudan, which still have some of the highest rates in the world. Across all 193 countries in 2019, the 90th-percentile rate is 74.2, not 66.9, and the gap is 70.9, not 63.4. The cohort makes the comparison fair and quietly makes the 2019 gap look smaller.

  2. Measure the spread with the 10th and 90th percentiles

    Percentiles are a standard way to ignore outliers. They also set aside the 11 most extreme countries at each end. In 2019 the worst-off countries in the cohort, the Central African Republic (110.1) and Sierra Leone (109.2), sit well above the 90th percentile of 66.9. Trimming them is defensible and invisible, and it keeps the most stubborn cases out of the headline number.

  3. A linear axis, and “gap” meaning a difference

    On an ordinary linear scale, falling rates pull together almost automatically, so the band funnels toward zero. The headline says the gap narrowed by 75 percent without saying which gap. As a difference it did; as a ratio it doubled, which is exactly what our AGAINST chart shows. A linear axis is the default nobody questions, which is why it persuades without looking like a choice.

  4. Add a panel showing that every country improved

    The second panel shows that all 112 countries cut their rate. That is progress, not convergence: countries can all improve while staying just as far apart. Placed under a chart about the gap, it lends the gap claim a sense of momentum it does not earn. Lesotho, the slowest improver, cut its rate by 54 percent, from 186.9 to 86.4, yet in 2019 it was still about 25 times the 10th-percentile country. This was the decision in our checkpoint that we were least comfortable with.

  5. Bin the improvements from 50 percent

    Following our checkpoint, the bins cover only 50 to 100 percent, so every bar reads as a large success and the longest bars sit at the top. Percentage cuts also treat a fall from 30 to 3 and a fall from 300 to 30 as the same 90 percent, even though one country still loses 10 times as many children. The bins are accurate, but the frame they create favors our argument.

2The case against

Now measure the gap as a ratio. In 1960 the 90th-percentile country’s rate was 9.5 times the 10th-percentile country’s. By 2019 it was 19.1 times. Both improved, but the country at the bottom cut its rate by 88 percent and the one at the top by 77 percent, so in relative terms the distance between them grew.

2.1How we made the AGAINST visualization

Each decision is scored from −2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest).

  1. Measure the gap as a ratio and call it “the divide”

    Dividing the 90th-percentile rate by the 10th is a legitimate measure of relative inequality, and public health researchers use it. It is also very sensitive to the denominator: the 10th-percentile country fell from 30.1 to 3.5 deaths per 1,000, so even modest progress at the bottom makes the ratio grow. Calling it the “child-survival divide” presents one of two reasonable measures as if it were the only one.

  2. Reuse the same 112 countries and percentiles as the FOR chart

    We built the ratio from exactly the same cohort and percentiles as Figure 1, so the two charts disagree only on the measure. This is an earnest decision. It removes any suspicion that the result comes from a different sample, and it makes the comparison between the two figures honest. It is also persuasive, because a reader who has seen Figure 1 cannot dismiss Figure 2 as cherry-picked data.

  3. “Doubled,” measured from 1960

    The ratio rose from 9.5 in 1960 to a peak of 23.1 in 1999, then fell 17 percent to 19.1 by 2019. The chart shows the peak, but the headline, the dashed reference line and the callout all compare 2019 with 1960, the lowest point. Measured from 1999, the divide has been narrowing for 20 years. Picking the baseline is the slant; every number on the chart is correct.

  4. Red for Sub-Saharan Africa only, and a ratio in the callout

    In the regional panel, Sub-Saharan Africa is the only region drawn in red, and every other country is gray, which casts one region as the problem. The callout states the difference as a ratio, 15.8 times, rather than as a gap of 56 deaths per 1,000. Both are true, but the ratio sounds far more alarming. This framing came straight from our checkpoint and is the most emotionally loaded choice in either figure.

  5. Let a 2019 snapshot stand in for a trend

    The regional panel shows a single year, so it cannot show whether anything is narrowing; it shows only that a gap exists. It also uses all 193 countries with a 2019 rate rather than the 112-country cohort, which brings in Nigeria, Niger, Chad and Somalia and widens Sub-Saharan Africa’s spread. Stacked under a time series, a snapshot borrows the series’ authority and reads as more evidence of a lasting divide.

3Same numbers, two stories

The disagreement fits in one table. Both visualizations start from the same two rates in the same two years. The FOR visualization reports their difference and the AGAINST visualization reports their ratio.

4Final reflection

At the checkpoint we leaned toward the FOR side. Absolute convergence is how most people read “the gap is narrowing,” and the AGAINST case depended on switching to ratios, which are sensitive to the tiny rates at the bottom. Building both sides out changed our minds about which was harder. The FOR visualization was easy: in a world where every country improved, nearly any honest default, such as a linear axis, a percentile band or a start date with good coverage, makes countries look like they are converging. The AGAINST visualization took more work, and what surprised us was how much of its force came from words and framing rather than geometry: “doubled” measured from 1960, “divide” for a ratio, and a single red region. Helen Kennedy and her co-authors argue that visualization conventions do rhetorical work by signaling objectivity. A matched cohort, a dashed baseline and a source line all signal rigor, and that rigor lends authority to whatever headline sits above them.

We now think of ethical analysis and visualization as making only the choices you would still defend to a reader who has seen the alternative. That suggests three tests. First, is the choice justified on grounds independent of the conclusion it produces? The matched cohort passes, because it compares like with like, while measuring from 1960 instead of the 1999 peak fails, because the start date was chosen for the answer it gives. Second, is the choice disclosed where readers actually look? Our percentile trimming is disclosed only in the source line, so it passes halfway. Third, does the chart invite a belief the data contradict? A second panel showing that every country improved invites the reader to infer convergence, which the panel cannot show. Ha-Kyung Kong and colleagues found that slanted titles shape what readers remember even when the chart itself is neutral, and our own most effective deceptions sat in titles, callouts and panel choices, not in the axes.

So is the gap narrowing? In absolute terms and in survival terms, clearly yes. In relative terms it widened from 1960 until about 1999 and has narrowed slightly since. Each of our visualizations misleads mainly through what it leaves out, and none of our individual techniques is unusual; each appears in published, respected graphics. That is why we don’t think a list of banned techniques can separate persuasion from deception. The line is crossed when the combination of choices leaves a reader with a belief they would abandon after seeing the table above.

5About the data

Data come from the course’s copy of the World Bank’s Human Development Indicators, 1960–2020 (light-and-salt/World-Bank-Data-by-Indicators), using the pre-cleaned aid-effectiveness/aid-effectiveness.csv file and its country metadata. The measure is the under-five mortality rate, deaths per 1,000 live births. It is a rate, not a count of deaths. Countries are the 217 entries with a World Bank region, not regional aggregates.

The matched cohort is the 112 countries with a rate in both 1960 and 2019. Its yearly 10th and 90th percentiles use nearest-rank quantiles, and all country summaries are unweighted. The regional panel uses all 193 countries with a 2019 rate. These methods and all figures match our checkpoint (vsinha99/dsc106-project2). The final charts were redrawn with D3. Code is in this repository (analysis/export.py, charts/chart.html), and live versions are available for Figure 1 and Figure 2.

References: Kennedy et al., “The Work That Visualization Conventions Do” (2016); Kong et al., “Frames and Slants in Titles of Visualizations on Controversial Topics” (CHI 2018); Burns et al., “From Invisible to Visible” (IEEE TVCG 2022); Lan et al., “Affective Visualization Design” (IEEE TVCG 2024).
