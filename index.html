Child Deaths Have Plunged Everywhere. Whether Countries Are Converging Depends on the Math.

We used one World Bank dataset to make two charts that disagree. Every number in both of them is correct.

By [Member 1], [Member 2], [Member 3] and [Member 4] [email1]@ucsd.edu · [email2]@ucsd.edu · [email3]@ucsd.edu · [email4]@ucsd.edu

Oct. 21, 2026

In 1960, the typical country among the 112 with records going back that far lost about 149 of every 1,000 children before their fifth birthday. By 2019 the figure was 15. Every one of those countries improved.

The harder question, and the one our group argued about, is whether countries moved closer together while they improved. We took the proposition below and made one chart for it and one against it, using the same countries, the same years and the same summary statistics.

Proposition

The gap in under-five mortality rates between countries is narrowing.

Each chart has a Show decision markers button. It numbers the spots on the chart that match the design decisions listed below it.

The case for

Look at the distance between countries in plain deaths per 1,000 births, and it shrinks dramatically. In 1960, the country at the 90th percentile lost 285 children per 1,000 and the country at the 10th percentile lost 30. In 2019 those figures were 66.9 and 3.5. The difference between them fell from 255 to 63.

How we made this chart

Each decision is scored from −2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest).

  1. Use only countries with data in both 1960 and 2019

    Keeping the same 112 countries in every year means countries entering the data partway through can’t move the trend. It also leaves out 81 countries, including Nigeria, Niger, Mali, Chad, Somalia and South Sudan, which still have some of the highest rates in the world. With all 193 countries that report for 2019, the 90th percentile would be 74.2 instead of 66.9. The choice is fair on paper, and it still makes 2019 look a little better for our side.

  2. Summarize with the 10th and 90th percentiles

    Percentiles are a normal way to handle outliers, and no reader is likely to question them. They also set aside the 11 most extreme countries at each end. In 2019 the Central African Republic (110.1) and Sierra Leone (109.2) sit far above the 90th percentile of 66.9, and the headline number never sees them.

  3. Plot raw rates on a linear axis

    When every rate falls, the distance between high and low rates shrinks almost by default, so the band funnels toward zero. The headline says the country gap narrowed by 75% and never says which kind of gap it means. Measured as a ratio it doubled, which is what our other chart shows. We left the axis as is because a linear scale never looks like a choice.

  4. Add a panel where every country improved

    All 112 countries cut their rate, and that is real progress. It says nothing about whether they moved closer together, since countries can all improve and stay just as far apart. Under the main chart, it makes the shrinking band feel like part of a bigger success story. Lesotho improved the least, falling from 186.9 to 86.4, and in 2019 its rate was still about 25 times the country at the 10th percentile. Of everything we kept from our checkpoint, this is the choice we’re least comfortable with.

  5. Count the declines in bins that start at 50%

    Every country cut its rate by more than half, so the bins run from 50% to 100% and every bar looks like a win. Percent declines also treat a drop from 30 to 3 the same as a drop from 300 to 30, even though the second country still loses ten times as many children.

The case against

Divide instead of subtract, and the picture flips. In 1960, the country at the 90th percentile had 9.5 times the rate of the country at the 10th. In 2019 it had 19.1 times. Both improved, but the bottom fell by 88% and the top by 77%, so the ratio between them grew.

How we made this chart

Each decision is scored from −2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest).

  1. Describe a ratio as inequality doubling

    Dividing one percentile by the other is a standard measure of relative inequality. It is also touchy, because the bottom number is tiny. The 10th percentile fell from 30.1 to 3.5, so even small improvements at the bottom push the ratio up. Our headline treats this one measure as if it settled the question.

  2. Keep the same countries and percentiles as Figure 1

    We built the ratio from exactly the same 112 countries and percentiles as the first chart, so the two charts disagree only because one subtracts and the other divides. This is the most honest thing about the chart. It also makes the chart harder to dismiss as cherry picked.

  3. Measure the change from 1960

    The ratio climbed from 9.5 in 1960 to 23.1 in 1999 and has fallen to 19.1 since. The chart shows the peak, but the headline, the dashed line and the annotation all compare 2019 with 1960, the low point. Starting in 1999 would show 20 years of countries moving closer. Every number on the chart is correct, and the starting year does the persuading.

  4. Color one region red

    In the bottom panel, Sub-Saharan Africa is the only region in red and everything else is gray, so the eye goes straight to it. The annotation calls its median 15.8 times Europe and Central Asia’s instead of saying it is 56 deaths per 1,000 higher. This came from our checkpoint, and it is the most emotional choice in either chart.

  5. Use a single year to argue about a trend

    The regional panel shows only 2019, so it can’t say anything about whether countries are converging. It also uses all 193 countries with 2019 data instead of the 112 in the top panel, which adds Nigeria, Niger, Chad and Somalia. Placed under a time series, it reads like more evidence of a trend.

Same data, two answers

Both charts start from the same two percentiles in the same two years. The first subtracts them and the second divides them.

What we learned

At the checkpoint we leaned toward the side arguing for the proposition. Most people hear “narrowing” as a shrinking difference, and the case against relied on ratios that swing around when the bottom rate is close to zero. Making both final charts changed how we saw the two sides. The case for was easy, because when every country improves, almost any default makes them look like they’re converging. The case against took longer, and most of its pull came from wording and color rather than from the lines themselves. Saying “doubled” from a 1960 baseline, calling a ratio inequality and coloring one region red did more work than any axis choice. Helen Kennedy and her coauthors describe how conventions like a clean baseline and a source line make a chart look objective, and we watched that happen with our own work. The fixed cohort and the dashed 1960 line made our most slanted headline look careful.

We now think ethical visualization means making choices you could defend to someone who has seen the alternative. Three questions helped us sort our own decisions. Would we have made the choice if it pointed the other way? The fixed cohort passes and the 1960 starting point for the ratio doesn’t. Can readers see the choice where they actually look? Our percentile trimming is mentioned only in the source line, so it half passes. Does the chart leave readers believing something the data contradict? The panel showing that every country improved invites a conclusion about convergence that it can’t support. Ha-Kyung Kong and colleagues found that slanted titles change what readers remember even when the chart is neutral. Our most effective tricks were in titles, annotations and panel choices too, and none of them touched the axes.

Our own answer to the proposition is mixed. In deaths per 1,000 births, or in the share of children who survive, countries are much closer than they were in 1960. As a ratio, they grew apart until about 1999 and have moved slightly closer since. Each chart misleads mostly by leaving the other measure out. We could not find a technique in either chart that respected publications avoid, so we don’t think a list of banned techniques can separate persuasion from deception. A chart crosses the line when its reader would change their mind after seeing the table above.

About the data

Data come from the World Bank’s Human Development Indicators, 1960 to 2020, in the cleaned copy linked from the assignment (light-and-salt/World-Bank-Data-by-Indicators, aid-effectiveness.csv and its country metadata). The measure is the under-five mortality rate, in deaths per 1,000 live births. It is a rate, not a count of deaths. Only countries are included, not regional aggregates.

Both charts use the 112 countries with data in 1960 and 2019. Percentiles use the nearest rank, and every country counts equally. The regional panel uses all 193 countries with 2019 data. These methods and numbers match our checkpoint (vsinha99/dsc106-project2). The final charts were redrawn in D3, and the code is in nahatav/dsc106-project2.

References: Kennedy et al., “The Work That Visualization Conventions Do” (2016). Kong et al., “Frames and Slants in Titles of Visualizations on Controversial Topics” (CHI 2018). Burns et al., “From Invisible to Visible” (IEEE TVCG 2022).
