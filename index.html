Convergence or divergence? Two readings of child mortality across countries, 1960 to 2019

[Member 1], [Member 2], [Member 3] and [Member 4] [email1]@ucsd.edu, [email2]@ucsd.edu, [email3]@ucsd.edu, [email4]@ucsd.edu

We examine whether child mortality has converged across countries, using the World Bank’s under-five mortality series for the 112 countries with data in both 1960 and 2019. Over that period the median rate fell from 149.4 to 15.0 deaths per 1,000 live births, and every country in the sample recorded a decline. Whether the distribution also narrowed depends on the measure. The absolute difference between the countries at the 90th and 10th percentiles fell by 75%, from 255.2 to 63.4 deaths per 1,000 live births, while the ratio between them rose from 9.5 to 19.1. We present one visualization built on each measure, document the design decisions that make each persuasive, and score each decision on a scale from deceptive to earnest.

Proposition

Under-five mortality rates are converging across countries.

1Visualization for the proposition

Figure 1 measures dispersion as the absolute difference between the countries at the 90th and 10th percentiles of under-five mortality. In 1960 those countries recorded 285.3 and 30.1 deaths per 1,000 live births. In 2019 they recorded 66.9 and 3.5. The difference fell from 255.2 to 63.4.

1.1Design decisions

Each decision is scored from −2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest).

  1. Restrict the sample to countries observed in 1960 and 2019

    A fixed sample prevents changes in coverage from producing an apparent trend. It also excludes 81 countries, including Nigeria, Niger, Mali, Chad, Somalia and South Sudan, which had some of the highest rates in 2019. Across all 193 countries reporting in 2019, the 90th percentile is 74.2 rather than 66.9. The restriction is methodologically sound, and it also lowers the 2019 upper bound in a way that favors the proposition.

  2. Summarize dispersion with the 10th and 90th percentiles

    Percentiles are a conventional way to limit the influence of outliers. They also exclude the 11 most extreme countries at each end of the distribution. In 2019 the Central African Republic (110.1) and Sierra Leone (109.2) lie well above the 90th percentile (66.9), so the countries with the slowest progress do not enter the headline figure.

  3. Plot rates on a linear scale

    On a linear scale, a uniform proportional decline reduces absolute differences, so the band narrows even when every country improves at the same percentage rate. The scale is the default and is unlikely to be read as a choice. On a logarithmic scale, the upper and lower bounds would appear to fall roughly in parallel.

  4. State the reduction as a percentage in the title

    The title reports a 75% reduction in the absolute difference between the two percentiles without naming the measure. Expressed as a ratio, the same two percentiles moved further apart over the period. Readers are likely to take “closer” as a general statement about inequality in child survival rather than a statement about one measure of it.

  5. Show every country in the sample

    Each of the 112 countries is drawn as a thin gray line behind the band, including those above the 90th percentile. This discloses the full distribution and allows a careful reader to see which countries the band leaves out. The lines are deliberately faint, which keeps attention on the band.

2Visualization against the proposition

Figure 2 measures dispersion as the ratio of the same two percentiles. The rate at the 90th percentile was 9.5 times the rate at the 10th percentile in 1960 and 19.1 times in 2019. Both percentiles declined, but the 10th percentile fell by 88% and the 90th by 77%, so the ratio increased.

2.1Design decisions

Each decision is scored from −2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest).

  1. Measure dispersion as a ratio and describe it as inequality

    The ratio of the 90th to the 10th percentile is a standard measure of relative inequality. It is also sensitive to its denominator. The 10th percentile fell from 30.1 to 3.5, so small absolute changes at the low end move the ratio substantially. The title presents this single measure as the state of inequality in child survival.

  2. Use the same sample and percentiles as Figure 1

    The ratio is computed from the same 112 countries and the same percentiles as Figure 1, so the two figures differ only in whether the percentiles are subtracted or divided. The result cannot be attributed to a different sample, and the comparison between the two figures is direct.

  3. Measure change from 1960

    The ratio rose from 9.5 in 1960 to a peak of 23.1 in 1999 and declined to 19.1 by 2019. The peak is labeled, but the title, the dashed reference line and the annotation all compare 2019 with 1960, the minimum of the series. Measured from 1999, the ratio fell by 17%.

  4. Draw the series in red against a dashed 1960 baseline

    Red is associated with risk and loss, and the dashed baseline makes the distance above the 1960 level the most salient feature of the chart. Both conventions are common in published graphics. Together they encourage reading the series as a deterioration, although both percentiles declined throughout the period.

  5. Restate the ratio as a relative risk for an individual child

    The annotation converts the ratio into the statement that a child in one country was 19.1 times as likely to die before age 5 as a child in another. The statement is correct. Framing it at the level of an individual child gives the ratio more emotional weight than the aggregate measure, and the annotation does not mention that both risks fell sharply.

3Comparison of the two measures

Figure 3 reports both measures for the same two percentiles. The absolute difference fell by 75% and the ratio rose by 102%. The difference in the probability of survival to age 5, which equals the absolute difference divided by 10, fell by the same proportion as the absolute difference.

4Reflection

At the checkpoint, our group favored the visualizations supporting the proposition, on the grounds that convergence is usually understood in absolute terms and that the ratio is unstable when the lower percentile approaches zero. Producing the final pair changed our view of which argument was harder to construct. When every country improves, most default choices, including a linear scale and a fixed sample, produce the appearance of convergence. The argument against required more deliberate choices, and its persuasive force came mainly from text and color: the 1960 baseline in the title, the description of a ratio as inequality, and the restatement of the ratio as a risk to an individual child. This is consistent with Kennedy et al., who argue that conventions such as clean baselines and cited sources give visualizations an appearance of objectivity. In our case, the fixed sample and the dashed reference line made the most slanted title in the project appear careful.

We define ethical analysis and visualization as the practice of making choices that could be defended to a reader who has seen the alternatives. Three questions were useful in applying this definition. The first is whether the choice would have been made if it favored the other side. The fixed sample meets this standard, and the 1960 baseline for the ratio does not. The second is whether the choice is disclosed where readers look. The use of percentiles is stated only in the source note, which is a partial disclosure. The third is whether the visualization encourages a belief the data do not support. Describing the ratio as inequality in child survival does so, because survival probabilities converged. Kong et al. found that slanted titles affect what readers recall even when the chart itself is neutral. In our work, the most effective persuasive choices were likewise made in titles and annotations rather than in the visual encoding.

Our own reading of the data is that child mortality converged in absolute terms and in survival probability, while relative dispersion increased until 1999 and has declined modestly since. Each visualization misleads chiefly by omitting the other measure. None of the techniques we used is unusual in published graphics, which leads us to conclude that persuasion and deception cannot be separated by a list of prohibited techniques. The distinction depends on whether the combination of choices leaves readers with a conclusion they would revise after seeing both measures.

5Data and methods

Data are from the World Bank’s Human Development Indicators, 1960 to 2020, in the cleaned copy linked from the assignment (light-and-salt/World-Bank-Data-by-Indicators, aid-effectiveness.csv and its country metadata). The indicator is the under-five mortality rate, deaths before age 5 per 1,000 live births. It is a rate and not a count of deaths. Regional and income aggregates are excluded.

The sample consists of the 112 countries with a value in both 1960 and 2019. Annual 10th and 90th percentiles are computed by the nearest rank method, and every country is weighted equally. These definitions follow our checkpoint (vsinha99/dsc106-project2). Figures were drawn with D3. Code is available at nahatav/dsc106-project2, with interactive versions of Figure 1 and Figure 2.
